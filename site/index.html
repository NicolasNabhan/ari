The problem

The procedure says one thing. The expert does another.

Every company has written procedures. But the people who do the job best follow a second set of rules that only lives in their heads: a scoring formula from Finance, a supplier you never trust with a deadline, an approval that always goes to the CFO. When they retire or move on, that knowledge walks out the door, and the next person learns it the hard way.

How Ari learns

Six steps, from watching to teaching.

This loop took us the longest to get right. The goal: learn the why behind an expert’s work without interrupting them every two minutes.

1 · Watch

Every click, every screen, what they read and for how long.

2 · Explain it to itself

Ari predicts each choice and tries to explain it from what it knows.

3 · Ask only if needed

Only when it isn’t sure and the answer matters to the next person.

4 · Sort the reason

Must follow, strong advice, or personal choice, and flag what’s unwritten.

5 · Check its guesses

At the end, it reads back what it was least sure about.

6 · Teach

A voice tutor for the next person, in the same workspace.

Step 1 · Watching

It sees what the expert does, and what they read.

Ari doesn’t just log clicks. It notices how someone works, and with eye tracking, what they looked at before they decided.

  • Every action becomes a step. Opening a request, asking for quotes, choosing a vendor, scoring, routing an approval: each one is turned into a decision with its options.
  • How they work, not just what. Pauses, what they checked first, who they contacted, and changes of mind become “how” notes on the decision.
  • Eye tracking with a normal webcam. A 9-dot calibration, then every glance is mapped to what’s on screen and merged into reading time. A real read is told apart from a passing glance.
  • Sharper questions. Because Ari saw Maria reading Apex’s late deliveries, it doesn’t ask a vague “why?”; it asks the right one.
  • Session recording. The whole screen session is recorded, so every step can be replayed.
  • Opt-in and private. Eye tracking runs entirely in the browser, only when the expert turns it on, with the mouse as a fallback.

Steps 2 & 3 · Asking why

It asks only when it can’t work it out, and it matters.

An apprentice that asks about everything is useless. Ari explains each choice to itself first, and stays quiet whenever it can.

  • It predicts first. Before the expert decides, Ari guesses what they’ll pick. A correct prediction is evidence; a surprise is a signal.
  • A confidence bar. If Ari can explain a choice with at least 70% confidence, from the procedure, the evidence or what it saw, it stays quiet.
  • Only what matters to the next person. A question is worth asking only if the answer would change how someone else should do the job.
  • Good manners. It holds questions while the expert is typing or on a call, offers a “tap to hear” mode, and asks at most one follow-up.
  • Answer by voice. The microphone opens by itself when Ari asks, with a live waveform, and typing works too.

Step 4 · Sorting knowledge

Every reason gets a type, a level, and a source.

Not every reason is a rule. Ari separates the guardrails the next person must keep from the personal touches they can do their own way.

  • Three levels. Must follow, strong advice, or your choice, from 25 reason types in six groups.
  • Unwritten rules. When the expert does something the written procedure doesn’t require, the decision gets an Unwritten rule label.
  • The knowledge behind the work. Finance’s 40/60 scoring formula is saved with where to find it: told by a person, a company document, a company system, public information, or a given.
  • In their own words. The expert’s answer is kept word for word, next to Ari’s own explanation and its confidence.

Step 5 · Checking its guesses

Before the expert leaves, Ari double-checks.

Every reason Ari worked out on its own is a guess. At the end of the session, it offers to read back the ones it’s least sure about, one by one, and the expert confirms or corrects them by voice. Or they get a short list, least certain first.

  • Least certain first, so the expert’s time goes where it matters.
  • Confirmed vs. best guess is shown on every card, forever.
  • Where their eyes went, per screen, with reading time, as a final summary.

The big picture

A job is more than one task. Ari learns the whole week.

Some knowledge only shows up across days, or lives in meetings and files rather than on screen. Ari pulls all of it together.

Week & schedule

Patterns you’d never see in a single day.

  • The expert’s whole week: meetings, focus time, recurring tasks and breaks, Monday to Friday. Breaks count too: 10 minutes on Wednesday, 15 on Thursday.
  • Trends across days. OfficeHub’s discount is 50% on Tuesday, 40% on Wednesday, 30% on Thursday, which is exactly why Maria orders supplies on Tuesday mornings.
  • Recurring slots and differences by weekday: the daily stand-up, Monday’s Finance sync, the Friday spend report.
  • Ari checks with the expert: “Is that why you always order supplies on Tuesday morning?” One tap to confirm.

  • Schedule first. Ari starts with the week, because it shapes the whole job: the fixed meetings, the breaks, and the patterns. A plan is ready on day one: your week, your day, and a guide for each recurring task.
  • Step by step. For each step: what to do, what the expert chose, why, and whether it’s a rule or just their style. The next button glows once Ari has said where to click.
  • Ask “why?” anytime. Ari answers in the expert’s own words, with what they read and the knowledge behind it.
  • “Show me.” Ari takes the cursor and does the step itself, the way the expert did, while explaining.
  • Speaks your language. Ask in Spanish and Ari keeps teaching in Spanish.
  • Stops mistakes before they happen. About to break a must-follow rule? Ari warns you first, out loud and on screen.
  • A written procedure, compiled. Everything Ari learned also becomes a step-by-step procedure the team can keep.
